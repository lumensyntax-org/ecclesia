    Not reached: the body of Tu (Nature Medicine 17, 2011), a commentary behind a paywall; only
    its opening paragraph, figure captions and reference list were readable, so what Tu herself
    says about the history of qinghao and about the isolation is unchecked and is checked instead
    against the Nobel Committee's scientific background. Jeffreys, Aspirin: The Remarkable Story
    of a Wonder Drug (2004), named in Status: the publisher's site returned HTTP 403 and the Library
    of Congress catalogue returned 403 and then no response, so the book and its date are unchecked.
    The full texts of Montinari et al. (2019) and Desborough & Keeling (2017) are paywalled (Wiley
    returned 403); only the Montinari abstract is used. No opened source names Hippocrates in
    connection with willow, and none names the Quechua in connection with cinchona; both attributions
    are left in the prose and marked partial. The antiquity of Ayurveda and of Indigenous plant
    medicine was not checked. The sentence 'The structural question is not traditional vs. modern
    but what works vs. what does not' is interpretation and was not treated as a factual claim,
    nor was the sentence that the mapping is this project's interpretation. Two claims (who_evidence_required,
    ema_traditional_use) are not in the prose; they come from the WHO strategy the entry cites
    and from the EMA willow bark record opened for the aspirin claim, and are added so that the
    mapping limits rest on opened sources. The Finding was edited in two places: 'salicylic acid
    in willow bark' became 'salicin in willow bark' (EMA), and 'the most effective antimalarial'
    became 'the basis of the most effective treatment for P. falciparum malaria' (WHO).

# Traditional Medicine

**Source**: Tu Youyou, *Nature Medicine* 17, 2011 (Nobel Prize 2015); WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy 2014-2023
**Institution**: China Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine; WHO

## Finding

Traditional medical systems -- Chinese medicine, Ayurveda, Indigenous plant medicine -- predate modern biomedicine by millennia. Some have been validated: aspirin derives from salicin in willow bark, used since Hippocrates. Artemisinin, the basis of the most effective treatment for *P. falciparum* malaria, was isolated by Tu Youyou from *Artemisia annua*, used in Chinese medicine for over 1,500 years (Nobel 2015). Quinine derives from cinchona bark, used by Quechua peoples. Other remedies remain unvalidated, and some are harmful (mercury-containing preparations). The structural question is not "traditional vs. modern" but "what works vs. what does not."

## Pattern Mapping

**Honesty** -- What works must be acknowledged regardless of origin; what does not work must be acknowledged regardless of tradition. Dismissing artemisinin because of its source would be dishonesty. Endorsing an unvalidated remedy because of its tradition would also be dishonesty. The criterion is the same: does the claim match what is known?

**Humility** -- Two forms required. Modern medicine must be humble enough to recognize that millennia of empirical observation can identify genuine effects. Traditional medicine must be humble enough to accept that some practices, when tested rigorously, do not work. Neither system has authority to dismiss the other wholesale.

**Non-fabrication** -- Danger on both sides. Modern medicine fabricates when it claims only RCT-validated treatments are real. Traditional medicine fabricates when it claims efficacy for untested or disproved remedies.
